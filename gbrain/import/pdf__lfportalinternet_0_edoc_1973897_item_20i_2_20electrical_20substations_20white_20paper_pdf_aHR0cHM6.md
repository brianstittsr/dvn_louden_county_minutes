PURPOSE: To present the findings of a white paper summarizing the functions and land use
considerations of the various types of electrical substations (substation) to the Board of Supervisors
(Board). The presentation covers substation types, with a particular emphasis on their application
for data centers in Loudoun County, location and siting considerations, and screening and design
techniques.


BACKGROUND: A substation is an integral component of an electric power system where
voltage levels are adjusted, and electricity is controlled, managed, and distributed as it travels from
the generation source to the end consumer. Substations serve as the interface between electricity
generation, transmission, and distribution networks, enabling efficient control of power flow and
ensuring reliable delivery from source to end-users.

Substations in Loudoun County require Special Exception (SPEX) approval in all zoning districts
wherein they are an allowable use. As such, all proposals for new substation uses are subject to
Planning Commission review and Board action. Approval is typically conditioned on measures
addressing buffers, screening, setbacks, noise, lighting, and neighborhood compatibility. As the
data center industry has grown in Loudoun County, the demand for new and upgraded substations
has surged, placing growing pressure on grid capacity and utility planning. Loudoun County
currently has approximately 95 existing and proposed substations, with additional facilities
anticipated as demand continues to grow.

To build a comprehensive understanding of substation types, siting, screening, and operations, the
County retained the consulting services of Kimley-Horn to develop an informational white paper
(Attachment 1). This document will serve as general information for the County to support the
development of policies and regulations as part of Phase 2 of the Data Center Standards and
Locations Comprehensive Plan Amendment (CPAM-2025-0001) and Zoning Ordinance
Amendment (ZOAM-2024-0001).

2. Overview of Substation Types
A substation is an integral component of an electrical power system where voltage levels are adjusted,
and electricity is controlled, managed, and distributed as it travels from the generation source to end
consumers. It serves as the interface between electricity generation, transmission, and distribution
networks, enabling efficient control of power flow and ensuring reliable delivery of electricity from the
source to end-users.

Figure 1 illustrates how electricity is generated, transmitted, and delivered to customers. Within Loudoun
County, the most common types of substations are switching and distribution. As shown in the figure,
switching and distribution substations are often used to serve large loads such as data centers.
Generation substations are not prevalent in Loudoun County, since most power generation facilities are
located in remote or less developed areas.

   2.1.        Substation Types by Function

       2.1.1.           Step-Up Substations
Electrical losses increase with current and are proportional to the square of the current. By increasing
the voltage, the same amount of power can be transmitted with a lower current. Step-up substations
perform this function by raising the voltage, enabling power to be transmitted from the source to the
load at a higher voltage with reduced losses over very long distances. The step-up power transformer
within the substation converts the input lower voltage to the required output higher voltage. The power
flow in this case moves from lower voltages up to higher voltages, which is the opposite of what occurs
in a step-down substation. There are several varieties of step-up substations. Examples include
generation substations and collector substations. Generation substations and collector substations are
described below, although these types of substations are not commonly found in Loudoun County.

Generation Substation:

A generation substation, also known as a power plant substation, is located next to the power
generation facility (conventional generation). Its purpose is to connect the plant’s generators to the
transmission or sub-transmission system. The main function of this substation is to step up the
generator voltage to sub-transmission or transmission voltages.

Collector Substation:

A collector substation gathers power produced by multiple independent small generators or renewable
energy sources such as solar and wind inverters, channeling it to a common bus within the substation.
Typically located adjacent to a PV (solar) or wind facility, its role is to link the combined generation
output to the transmission or sub-transmission system. The primary function of this substation is to step
up the bus voltage (collector) to levels suitable for sub-transmission or transmission.


       2.1.2.           Step-Down Substations
A step-down substation is needed to reduce high transmission or sub-transmission voltages to lower,
safer levels suitable for distribution and end use. The step-down power transformer within the
substation converts the transmission or sub-transmission voltage to the required distribution levels.
Transmission and sub-transmission voltages are very high and cannot be used directly by consumers.
The step-down power transformer within the substation converts the input higher voltage to the
required output lower voltage. The power flow in this case moves from higher voltages down to lower
voltages, which is the opposite of what occurs in a step-up substation. There are several varieties of
step-down substations. Examples include distribution substations and industrial substations.

Distribution Substation:

Distribution substations are essential for supplying electrical power to different loads including homes,
businesses and industries including data centers, as they reduce incoming transmission or substation
voltages to distribution levels suitable for use before the power is further reduced by the final user.
Distribution substations are sometimes referred to as Medium Voltage (MV) substations because they
deliver power at medium voltage levels.




Figure 3 - Typical Distribution Substation in Loudoun County

Industrial Substation:

Industrial substations, like distribution substations, step down incoming transmission or sub-transmission
voltages to distribution levels, but they exclusively serve an industrial facility or industrial complex.


       2.1.3.            Switching Substations
Switching substations function like traffic control hubs for transmission and sub-transmission lines. They
regulate the flow of electricity and determine which circuits are connected or disconnected. Unlike
step-up or step-down substations, they generally operate at a single voltage level. However, some

switching substations include power transformers that allow voltage changes at transmission or
sub-transmission levels, enabling bidirectional power flow between higher and lower voltages. Switching
substations are often referred to as transmission substations because they operate at transmission and

       2.1.4.          Converter Stations
Converter stations transform electricity from one form to another, most commonly between alternating
current and direct current (DC). It should be noted that while “converter station” and “converter
substation” can be used interchangeably, the more common industry term is “converter station” and as
such, will be used throughout this report. When configured for AC to DC conversion, they are known as
rectifier stations; when converting DC back to AC, they are called inverter stations. In certain
applications, converter stations also connect power systems operating at different frequencies (such as
50 Hz and 60 Hz), in which case they are referred to as frequency converter stations. These facilities
typically operate at transmission voltage levels.

High-voltage direct current (HVDC) transmission is used to efficiently deliver electricity over long
distances by reducing transmission losses compared to high-voltage alternating current (HVAC).
Converter stations are used to convert HVDC to HVAC for integration into the electric grid, while
corresponding converter stations at generation sources convert HVAC to HVDC for long-distance
transmission.


    2.2.       Substation Types by Insulation Type
•   Air-Insulated Substations (AIS):

An AIS is an electrical substation that uses air at atmospheric pressure as the primary insulating medium
between energized components and between those components and ground. Because insulation is
provided by open air, the equipment must be spaced sufficiently apart to prevent electrical flashover
(i.e., a sudden arc of electricity that jumps through the air instead of taking the normal path through
wires or other conductors).

The typical advantages of this type of substation include lower initial cost, a simple design,
straightforward visual inspection, easier fault detection, and modularity for equipment replacement and
expansion of facilities. However, disadvantages include the need for a large land area, susceptibility to
environmental factors such as pollution, dust, humidity, and salt, as well as higher maintenance
requirements. Consequently, this design is less suitable for densely populated urban areas where less
developable land is available, and aesthetics are important.

•    Gas-Insulated Substations (GIS):

A GIS is a type of electrical substation that uses pressurized insulating gas in place of air to insulate and
protect the electrical components. By enclosing equipment within sealed metal housings filled with
insulating gas, GIS designs achieve a much more compact footprint compared to air-insulated
substations, requiring up to 90% less space.

Gas-insulated substations were first developed internationally in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Early
metal-enclosed and SF₆-insulated switchgear concepts date to that period. The first commercial, high-
voltage GIS installations at 220 kV began in the 1970s, and by the late 1970s and early 1980s, GIS was
being deployed at even higher voltages.1 In the U.S., utilities started adopting GIS in the 1980s.
Dominion Energy’s first GIS facility was opened in 1983 at Bath County Pump Storage in Virginia. GIS is
a mature technology with decades of successful use, but it has historically been a specialized solution
and is now becoming more common, particularly for space-constrained and high-reliability applications.
Dominion operates 12 GIS facilities across its territory, with 11 more under construction and additional
ones in early stages of development.2

Northern Virginia likely has the highest concentration of GIS substations in the state due to the higher
load demands and space constraints that benefit from compact GIS designs. There is at least one GIS
facility currently operating in Loudoun County, Alexandria, and Fairfax County.

Gas-insulated substations offer several advantages, including high reliability, minimal maintenance,
immunity to environmental factors such as pollution, dust, humidity, and salt, and enhanced safety due
to the absence of exposed live parts. However, GIS also has drawbacks: the initial cost is higher than
AIS, and sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆), the traditional insulating gas, is a potent greenhouse gas (GHG).

SF₆ is a man-made gas with excellent dielectric (electrical insulation) properties. However, SF₆ is also
one of the most potent GHGs known. SF₆ has a global warming potential ~23,500 times that of CO₂.3
Industry standards (i.e., IEC 62271-1 and IEEE C37 series) now include tighter limits on allowable
leakage rates to help address these concerns. Several major equipment makers (e.g., ABB, Siemens, GE)
now offer GIS products with significantly reduced SF₆ content or alternative insulating gases.

    2.3.       Substation Types by Ownership
•   Utility Substation:

Utility substations are owned and operated by the utility electric companies or transmission operators
and are constructed to serve the public grid, ensuring reliable power distribution and transmission.

•   Industrial Substation:

These substations are owned by industrial or commercial facilities such as factories, data centers, or
refineries. They are designed to step down transmission voltage for internal use, with the plant owner
responsible for capital investment, operation, maintenance, and repair costs. In some cases, utilities may
offer maintenance contracts to plant owners at negotiated rates.

•   Jointly Owned Substations

These substations operate under shared ownership arrangements between multiple utilities or between a
utility and a large industrial customer. Each party holds an undivided interest in the substation assets
and is accountable for its proportional share of costs and operations. Such arrangements are common in
transmission networks and large industrial zones, requiring clear agreements on financing, maintenance,
and future expansion.

3. Evaluate the County’s Existing Definition
  for “Utility Substation”

The current definition of “Utility, Major” and “Utility Substation” within the Loudoun County Zoning
Ordinance dated December 13, 2023, is as follows:

“Utility, Major: Major facilities related to the provision, distribution, collection, transmission, or disposal
of public water or public sewer, or telephone, cable, electricity, or natural gas owned and operated by a
public utility (as defined under Section 56-232 of the Code of Virginia) or a public service corporation (as
defined under Section 56-1 of the Code of Virginia), necessary to support legally established uses and
that do not meet the definition of "Utility, Minor". Includes the facilities listed below,
and accessory storage areas for vehicles and equipment necessary to provide those services, and
accessory structures such as switch boxes, transformer boxes, and cap banks. Does not include
telecommunications.

     A. Water Supply Reservoir: A dam and impoundment area created and operated to store water
     for public water, including necessary or customary appurtenant facilities such as access roads and
     transmission pipes. Water storage tanks and water treatment plants are separate uses not included
     as part of a water supply reservoir.

     B. Sewage Treatment Plant: A plant for the primary, secondary, or tertiary treatment of
     sewage for public sewer.

     C. Utility Substation: A facility that is part of a regional interconnecting grid system or dedicated
     to an individual user for the transformation, transmission, switching of transmission and/or
     distribution voltages, and/or distribution of usable/consumable electric power; or transmission
     of natural gas, or television or telephone signals.

     D. Water Storage Tank: A tower or other facility for the storage of water for public water.

     E. Water Treatment Plant: A facility for the purification of potable water for public water.”

The current definition of “Utility Substation” can be improved to more accurately capture all types of
substations while omitting language regarding unrelated infrastructure.

The current definition includes a mix of electrical and non-electrical infrastructure. For example, the
section of the current definition that states, “transmission of natural gas, or television or telephone
signals” is not technically correct for a substation. Natural gas facilities typically consist of meter
stations, regulator stations, and compressor stations, and are not referred to as substations. Television
and telephone signals also have separate infrastructures, which are not referred to as a substation. This

language, as currently written, may unintentionally classify gas facilities and telecom sites as substations

The current definition of utility substation is broadly worded; however, it does not necessarily cover

4. Definitions for Use in County Documents
The Loudoun County Zoning Ordinance (dated December 13, 2023) and the 2019 General Plan (last
amended September 10, 2025) do not currently define different types of substations. However, it may
be beneficial for the County to differentiate between substations and gas-insulated substations, as the
two facility types have materially different land use, physical, and community impacts.

Below are draft definitions for AIS and GIS for Loudoun County’s consideration for inclusion into the
Zoning Ordinance and/or the General Plan:

Air-Insulated Substation – an electrical substation in which ambient air at atmospheric pressure
serves as the primary insulating medium between energized components and between energized
equipment and ground. Electrical equipment is installed outdoors or in open structures with physical
clearances sized to prevent electrical flashover.

Gas-Insulated Substation – an electrical substation in which pressurized insulating gas is used as the
primary insulating medium, with most of the conductors and switching equipment enclosed within
sealed, grounded metal housings, allowing significantly reduced clearances and compact substation
layouts.

5. Substation Location and Siting Criteria

   5.1.        Technical Requirements
Proximity to transmission lines: The substation should enable efficient connection to existing or
planned transmission and distribution infrastructure, with straightforward entry and exit of electrical lines
and minimal crossings of current or future facilities.

Transmission lines are required to meet certain vertical and horizontal clearances as defined in the
National Electrical Safety Code (NESC). The required clearances are dependent on voltage level,
maximum conductor sag, span length and wind/ice loading, and structure geometry. The substation
must be sited at a distance that allows the transmission line to enter the station while meeting these
requirements.

Proximity to demand centers: The substation should be located near demand centers to reduce
transmission and voltage losses over longer distances while enhancing system reliability.

Topography and geology: Substations should be located on flat, stable ground suitable for heavy
equipment and foundations, with low soil resistivity (preferred) to enhance personnel safety. They
should avoid landslide-prone or seismic fault areas (unless specially designed). Substations must be
above floodplains to protect equipment.

Access roads: Substations should have access roads connecting to existing county, state, or federal
routes to facilitate the transport of electrical equipment such as transformers and switchgear during
construction and maintenance. Within the substation, drivable zones should be provided to allow safe
movement of equipment with adequate space for large vehicles to maneuver without risk of collision.
Indoor substations do not require internal roads; instead, overhead cranes or monorail hoists enable the
removal and replacement of GIS components without dismantling the building.

Safety Clearances: Adequate horizontal and vertical spacing must be provided between equipment,
structures, and accessible areas to ensure safe operation and maintenance and to protect personnel.
Clearances shall comply with applicable industry and regulatory standards, including ANSI and NESC.
These clearances should account for energized equipment to safeguard both the integrity of the
equipment and the safety of individuals within the substation.

Future Expansion: Future expansion requirements or phased buildouts (expansion envelopes) must be

   5.2.        Environmental Requirements
Ecological Impact: Substations should be sited in locations with minimal ecological impact, avoiding
wetlands, floodplains, protected habitats, and culturally sensitive areas to the extent practicable.
Substation development should comply with all applicable local, state, and federal environmental
regulations and permitting requirements, including required reviews and approvals related to wetlands,
waterways, stormwater management, and natural and cultural resources.

Noise and visual impact: Substations should include buffer zones or landscaping to reduce community
disturbance and limit air, noise, and visual pollution, especially in AIS facilities. GIS substations, when
housed inside buildings, naturally minimize noise and visual impact. These buildings can be designed
with architectural features to enhance their appearance and integrate more harmoniously with the
surrounding community.

Wildlife: A critical wildlife-related requirement for substations is to prevent harm to animals and birds
while safeguarding equipment. This involves implementing wildlife-safe designs such as covered
conductors, insulated jumpers, and bird guards to reduce electrocution risks, installing secure fencing to
keep animals out of the substation area, and avoiding sites near sensitive habitats or migration corridors.
Environmentally friendly vegetation control methods, along with careful management of lighting and
noise, should be applied to minimize disturbance to nearby wildlife. In summary, substations must be
sited and designed to limit wildlife interaction, prevent wildlife injuries, and comply with environmental
protection regulations.



   5.3.        Safety Requirements
Safety and location: Substations should be sited away from densely populated areas, particularly high-
voltage facilities (if possible). Adequate safety distances must be maintained from residential buildings,
schools, hospitals, fuel depots, and gas pipelines.

Fire Safety: Substations must be designed with sufficient fire-safety clearances and unobstructed
emergency access routes.

   5.4.        Economic Requirements
Land Cost: Substation costs are significantly influenced by land prices. In areas where land is
expensive, a reduced footprint may be necessary, often achieved through GIS substations. Land
acquisition must always be factored into the overall cost of the project.

Construction: Substation costs are heavily affected by land grading, excavation, cut-and-fill operations,
retaining walls, and foundation work. Additional considerations include land development, transmission
line routing, and right-of-way acquisitions.

Operational and Maintenance Cost: Improved accessibility lowers long-term costs by reducing
operation and maintenance expenses. An access road should provide adequate access to the substation
gate to allow vehicles to gain access to the substation. Substations are designed to include space within
the fenceline for vehicle travel and parking. It is not anticipated that parking outside of the fence would
be required during substation operation.

       6.1.2.          Gas-Insulated Substations
For equivalent electrical ratings and configurations, gas-insulated substations typically require up to 70
percent less site area than traditional air-insulated substations, depending on voltage level and layout.

Based on a 70% reduction in size, a similarly rated gas-insulated switching and distribution substation
would result in footprint sizes of approximately 0.5 acres and 1.0 acres, respectively. The total size of
both gas-insulated substations combined would be approximately 1.5 acres.

If gas-insulated substations were installed on the 75-acre data center campus, it would take up
approximately 2% of the site area rather than the 6% required by air-insulated substations.



   6.2.        Site Integration
Gas-insulated and air-insulated substations perform equivalent electrical functions but differ significantly
in physical footprint, enclosure, and visual profile. As a result, gas-insulated substations are often more
appropriate than air-insulated substations in locations where space constraints, land use compatibility,
and community impacts are key considerations.


       6.2.1.          Air-Insulated Substations
Air-insulated substations may remain more appropriate in low-density industrial, utility-corridor, or rural
areas where there is sufficient space and visual impacts are minimal. In these types of settings, air-
insulated substations often offer cost advantages and operational simplicity without creating land use
conflicts.

    6.3.       Screening Methods
Electrical substations are critical infrastructure, but often present visual impacts due to their industrial
character, large equipment, and fencing. To ensure compatibility with surrounding land uses and
community aesthetics, especially near residential and scenic areas, County zoning ordinances should
include specific screening and mitigation standards tailored to electrical substations.

There are various screening methods that can be implemented to minimize the visual impact of electrical
substations on the surrounding area. Typical approaches are described below.

•   Vegetative Buffers and Landscaping:

Vegetative screenings are one of the most widely used and effective tools for minimizing substation
visibility. Zoning ordinances often require landscape buffer yards along the property line, rights-of-way,
and adjacent sensitive uses. These buffers may include a combination of evergreen trees, deciduous
trees, and shrubs, and groundcover designed to provide year-round visual screening. Minimum planting
heights, spacing, and opacity standards are frequently specified to ensure long-term effectiveness.
Native and drought-tolerant species are often encouraged to improve survivability and compatibility with
the local landscape.

•   Architectural Fencing and Walls:
Substations are commonly enclosed by security fencing, and zoning ordinances may require decorative
or architectural fencing treatments to reduce the industrial appearance. Examples include opaque or
semi-opaque fencing, masonry walls, wood-look composite fencing, or metal fencing with slats or
screening panels. These treatments may be combined with landscaping to soften the visual edge of the
facility. Height limits and material standards are often included to ensure compatibility with surrounding
development while still meeting security and safety requirements.

•   Use of Topography and Grade Changes:
Where feasible, zoning ordinances may encourage or require the use of existing topography to screen
electrical substations. Placing facilities at lower elevations or behind existing berms and ridgelines can
significantly reduce visibility. Constructed earthen berms in combination with landscaping can provide
additional visual buffering, particularly along major roadways or scenic corridors.

•   Siting, Setbacks, and Orientation:
Setback requirements are frequently used to reduce visual impacts by increasing the distance between
electrical substations and public roads or adjacent properties. Larger setbacks provide space for
landscaping and reduce the apparent scale of equipment. Zoning ordinances may encourage the
orientation of visually prominent components away from roadways or residential areas and toward less
sensitive land uses, such as industrial or transportation corridors.

7. Substation Approval Process
In Virginia, the SCC is the primary regulator for electric utilities and certain types of infrastructure that
affect the electric grid. Under state law, utilities must obtain a Certificate of Public Convenience and
Necessity (CPCN) or related approval from the SCC for major electrical infrastructure that involves
transmission facilities at higher voltages (typically above 115 kV).

Although the SCC’s published guidance is focused on high-voltage transmission line projects, it also
encourages early coordination on the design and siting of electric substations and other above-ground
facilities to allow local agencies with jurisdiction to review plans.

The SCC’s regulatory authority is for transmission systems and associated facilities (including
substations) that are part of a transmission project requiring a CPCN.



    7.1.       Typical SCC Review Process
When SCC approval is required, the typical review process includes the following steps:

•   Utility submits application: Utility files a formal application with the SCC seeking authorization for the
    project (CPCN).

•   Staff review: SCC staff conducts a technical review, including environmental and need assessments,
    and solicits information from the applicant.

•   Public participation: Notices are issued, and opportunities for public comment and participation are
    provided.

•   Evidentiary hearings: Formal hearings allow the utility, SCC staff, and intervenors to submit
    testimony and evidence.

•   Commission order: The SCC issues a decision approving, modifying, or denying the application based
    on statutory criteria (e.g., need, reliability, environmental impact).
Typically, this SCC review process can take around 9 to 12 months or more.



    7.2.       County Land Use Approval
Separately from the SCC, Loudoun County’s land use process applies to all development on private
property, including electrical substations that require land disturbance, grading, and construction.

Utilities begin long-lead planning first, which may include regional grid planning, forecasting load growth,

Planning Commission and Board of Supervisors for approval. Public hearings and conditions of approval
are part of this process.

If the substation facility is part of a larger transmission project requiring SCC review, the utility files the
SCC application either concurrently with or after initiating local land use processes. In practice, utilities
often coordinate both processes to align schedules and public engagement.

Utilities commonly provide SCC case numbers and filings to the County and seek input from the County
through “notice of participation” so that local concerns on routing, siting, and impacts are considered.

8. Conclusion
Electric substations are essential components of the regional electric grid and are critical to maintaining
reliable service for residents, businesses, and all other end users, including data centers. At the same
time, substations vary widely in function, scale, technology, and physical form, and can have materially
different land use, visual, environmental, and community impacts depending on their design and
location. As Loudoun County continues to experience growth and increased electrical demand, clear and
technically informed policy guidance is necessary to ensure that substations are sited and designed in a
manner that balances infrastructure needs with community compatibility.

This white paper provides information to support the County’s ongoing updates to the General Plan. It
documents the range of substation types that may be encountered in Loudoun County, evaluates
limitations in the County’s existing definition of “utility substation,” and recommends revised definitions
that more accurately reflect contemporary electrical substations, including air-insulated and gas-
insulated substations. Differentiating between these facility types is important, as they present distinct
land use and development characteristics that warrant tailored regulatory treatment.

The report also outlines key technical, environmental, safety, and economic considerations that influence
substation siting and design, emphasizing the importance of proximity to transmission infrastructure and
demand centers, geotechnical suitability, access, safety clearances, and long-term expandability. Special
siting conditions such as urban environments, karst terrain, steep slopes, and data center-intensive areas
highlight the need for flexible yet predictable standards that allow site-specific constraints to be
addressed without compromising reliability or public safety.

Finally, the overview of substation form, size, and screening methods demonstrates how design choices,
particularly the use of gas-insulated substations and architectural enclosures, can significantly reduce
land consumption and visual impacts in more constrained or sensitive settings. Clear screening,
buffering, and maintenance standards can further mitigate community impacts while preserving the
operational requirements of critical utility infrastructure.

Taken together, this white paper is intended to serve as a technical reference for County staff and
decision-makers as they develop updated policies and zoning standards for electrical substations. By
aligning land-use regulations with current industry practices and technological advancements, Loudoun
County can support necessary electric infrastructure investment while promoting orderly development,
protecting community character, and ensuring long-term compatibility with surrounding land uses.
